Here are the biggest “dragons” hiding in the path to shipping a humane RSVP reader, organized by function and backed by the research and specs we’ve discussed:

1. Human‑comprehension limits – Mechanical and cognitive constraints cap sustainable reading speed around 500–600 wpm; pushing beyond that degrades verbatim comprehension  . Designing around this hard limit is essential; you can’t paper over it with clever code.
2. Loss of preview and re‑read – RSVP eliminates parafoveal preview and regressions. Readers naturally regress 10–15 % of the time ; preventing that produces anxiety and preference for traditional reading . Without robust flick‑back, pause and context‑reveal, many users will abandon the app.
3. Tokenization and boundary heuristics – Tokenizing for pacing is far trickier than splitting on spaces. You need deterministic handling of abbreviations, initials, decimals, ellipses, em/en dashes and closing punctuation. Mis‑classifying titles (“Dr.”, “No.”), context abbreviations (“etc.”, “Jan.”), decimals (“3.14.”), ellipses and dash‑separated clauses can wreck the rhythm. There’s no off‑the‑shelf rulebook for RSVP pacing; you must write and maintain your own tables and decision logic.
4. Cleanup vs. meaning – Pasted articles are full of URLs, bracketed citations, newsletter boilerplate, cookie prompts and share navigation. Removing these debris is necessary to avoid RSVP potholes, but over‑aggressive cleanup can silently delete meaningful sentences or fuse headings into prose. You need idempotent cleanup rules and an audit trail of every removal. Designing “high‑confidence” removal lists and repair heuristics is a time‑consuming, perpetual edge‑case hunt.
5. Phrase‑chunking accuracy – Skilled readers process text in syntactic and semantic phrases . Turning word tokens into phrase units requires detecting noun phrases, verb phrases and familiar expressions, keeping proper names and phrasal verbs intact, and deciding when to fall back to single words for code, formulas or dense legal text. Mistakes here shatter meaning and break the trance.
6. Adaptive pacing – A fixed words‑per‑minute slider is crude. The engine must vary timing based on word length, rarity, punctuation, clause boundaries and numbers . Getting these heuristics wrong leads to jarring speed changes or monotony; tuning them requires telemetry and iteration.
7. User acceptance and fatigue – Even with equal comprehension, people often dislike RSVP because it feels more demanding and reduces blinking . A large portion of potential users will drop out unless you prioritize comfort over speed, build trust through instant re‑read, and set expectations around completion rather than superhuman speeds .
8. Cross‑language and domain diversity – English‑centric heuristics break on other languages (e.g., Spanish abbreviations, German compound nouns) or domains (math notation, programming code, legal references). Supporting other languages or specialized content will multiply the edge cases and require additional rule sets.
9. Marketing and expectation management – Many earlier RSVP products failed because they promised “3× faster reading” and delivered frustration. Overselling invites backlash; the product must be framed around helping users finish articles comfortably, not defying physiology.
10. Maintenance burden – Every new content source (newsletters, PDFs, blogs) and every rare corner case discovered by users will force you to update the cleanup lists, boundary tables and phrase rules. Without disciplined specification and automated tests, the system will become brittle over time.

Addressing these dragons requires a disciplined spec (as you’ve drafted), rigorous testing and telemetry, and honest messaging. They aren’t fatal, but ignoring them will sink the product.
